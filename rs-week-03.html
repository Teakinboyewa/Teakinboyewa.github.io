Landsat

The Landsat program, its sensors and products, and how to download and prepare a scene in QGIS.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Describe the history of the Landsat program and why its long record matters for forests.
  • Identify the bands of Landsat 8 and 9 and what each is used for.
  • Explain Landsat's orbit, revisit time, and the path/row system.
  • Tell the Collection 2 product levels and tiers apart, and read a Landsat file name.
  • Download Level-2 surface reflectance data from USGS EarthExplorer.
  • Stack bands, apply scale factors, and build band composites in QGIS.

Lecture notes

1. The Landsat program

Landsat is a joint program of NASA and the U.S. Geological Survey (USGS). It holds the longest continuous record of the Earth's land surface from space.

  • 1972: Landsat 1 (first called ERTS-1) launches.
  • 1984: Landsat 5 launches. It operated until 2013, nearly 29 years.
  • 1999: Landsat 7 launches. In 2003 its Scan Line Corrector failed, leaving wedge-shaped gaps (stripes) in every image since.
  • 2008: USGS makes the entire Landsat archive free and open. Use of the data grows dramatically.
  • 2013: Landsat 8 launches with the OLI and TIRS sensors.
  • 2021: Landsat 9 launches with nearly identical sensors (OLI-2 and TIRS-2).

Because the record goes back more than 50 years, Landsat is the main source for studying long-term forest change, such as deforestation, regrowth, fire, and harvest. Well-known global forest-loss maps are built from Landsat.

2. Orbit and coverage

  • Landsat 8 and 9 fly in a sun-synchronous, near-polar orbit about 705 km up, crossing the equator at about 10:00 a.m. local time.
  • Each satellite images the same place every 16 days. The two are offset by 8 days, so together they revisit every 8 days.
  • Each scene covers about 185 km × 180 km.
  • Scenes are organized in the Worldwide Reference System-2 (WRS-2). A path follows the satellite's ground track and a row is the position along it, so each scene has a fixed path/row number.

3. Landsat 8 and 9 bands

The Operational Land Imager (OLI) records nine reflective bands. The Thermal Infrared Sensor (TIRS) records two thermal bands.

4. Collection 2: levels and tiers

USGS processes all Landsat data into Collection 2. You will meet two processing levels:

  • Level-1 products are calibrated and placed on the map, but they still include the effects of the atmosphere (top-of-atmosphere values).
  • Level-2 products are corrected for the atmosphere. They provide surface reflectance (SR) for the reflective bands and surface temperature (ST) from the thermal band. Use Level-2 for almost all analysis in this course, especially when you compare images from different dates.

Each scene also gets a tier. Tier 1 scenes meet the highest standards of geometric and radiometric quality and are suited to time-series analysis. Tier 2 scenes do not meet those standards, often because of heavy cloud cover.

A file name tells you almost everything about a scene. For example:

A Level-2 download also includes the surface temperature band (ST_B10), quality bands such as QA_PIXEL, and a metadata file ending in _MTL.txt.

6. Scale factors

To save space, Level-2 values are stored as integers. To get real values, apply the Collection 2 scale factors:

  • Surface reflectance = DN × 0.0000275 − 0.2
  • Surface temperature (kelvin) = DN × 0.00341802 + 149.0. Subtract 273.15 to get °C.

A value of 0 means "no data" (fill). After scaling, most reflectance values fall between 0 and 1.

Tip: when you only display a composite, scaling makes no difference, because QGIS stretches the colors anyway. You must scale when you compare values, calculate indices, or combine images from different dates.

7. Clouds and the QA_PIXEL band

Clouds and their shadows hide the surface, and they are the biggest problem in optical remote sensing. The QA_PIXEL band flags each pixel using individual bits of its value. For example, bit 3 marks cloud, bit 4 marks cloud shadow, bit 5 marks snow, bit 6 marks clear, and bit 7 marks water. In this lab you will look at the band. In Week 9, you will use it to mask clouds automatically in Google Earth Engine.

Key terms

OLI / TIRS
The Operational Land Imager and the Thermal Infrared Sensor on Landsat 8. Landsat 9 carries OLI-2 and TIRS-2.
WRS-2 path/row
The grid system that gives each Landsat scene a fixed location ID.
Collection 2
The current USGS processing of the whole Landsat archive.
Level-1
Calibrated, georeferenced data that still include atmospheric effects.
Level-2
Atmospherically corrected surface reflectance and surface temperature.
Tier 1
Scenes of the highest quality, suited to time-series analysis.
Scale factor
The multiplier and offset that turn stored integers into physical values.
QA_PIXEL
A quality band whose bits flag clouds, shadows, snow, water, and clear pixels.
Layer stack
Several single-band images combined into one multiband image.

Lab 3: Download and prepare a Landsat 9 scene

In this lab, you download a real Landsat scene of your study area, prepare it in QGIS, and compare band combinations.

You will need: QGIS, a free USGS account (register at ers.cr.usgs.gov), and about 1 GB of free disk space.

Part A. Find and download a scene

New to this? See the starter guides: Download Landsat from EarthExplorer · Organize your files and folders
  1. Log in to EarthExplorer.
  2. In the Search Criteria tab, find your study area by typing an address or place, or by clicking on the map to draw an area.
  3. Set a date range in the growing season, for example June through August of last year. Set cloud cover to 0–10%.
  4. In the Data Sets tab, choose Landsat › Landsat Collection 2 Level-2 › Landsat 8-9 OLI/TIRS C2 L2. Click Results.
  5. Use the footprint and preview icons to find a clear Tier 1 scene that covers your study area. Write down its full product name.
  6. Click the download icon. You can download the whole bundle or just these files: SR_B2 to SR_B7, ST_B10, QA_PIXEL, and MTL.txt. Put them in a new folder.

Part B. Stack and clip the bands

  1. In QGIS, open Raster › Miscellaneous › Build Virtual Raster.
  2. For Input layers, select the files SR_B2 through SR_B7, in order. Check Place each input file into a separate band. Save the output as landsat_stack.vrt.
  3. Note how the bands are numbered in the stack:
    Stack band123456
    Landsat bandB2 BlueB3 GreenB4 RedB5 NIRB6 SWIR 1B7 SWIR 2
  4. A full scene is large. Draw a polygon of your study area, or reuse one from Week 2. Then run Raster › Extraction › Clip Raster by Mask Layer on the stack. Save the result as landsat_clip.tif.

Part C. Build band composites

  1. Open Layer Properties › Symbology for landsat_clip and choose Multiband color.
  2. Using the stack numbering above, make natural color (Landsat 4-3-2, so stack bands 3, 2, 1). Under Min / Max Value Settings, choose Cumulative count cut (2–98%) to improve contrast.
  3. Make color infrared (Landsat 5-4-3, so stack bands 4, 3, 2) and agriculture (Landsat 6-5-2, so stack bands 5, 4, 1).
  4. To compare the composites, right-click the layer and choose Duplicate Layer, so each composite is its own layer. Export a picture of each with Project › Import/Export › Export Map to Image.

Part D. Turn numbers into reflectance and temperature

  1. Use the Identify tool to click a forest pixel and note the value of stack band 4 (NIR). Apply the scale factor by hand: DN × 0.0000275 − 0.2.
  2. Open Raster › Raster Calculator and compute NIR reflectance with "landsat_clip@4" * 0.0000275 - 0.2. Save it as nir_reflectance.tif and check that its values are mostly between 0 and 1.
  3. Record the reflectance of forest, water, and bare soil in the NIR. Compare these values with your Sentinel-2 measurements from Week 1.
  4. Optional: compute surface temperature in °C from ST_B10 with "ST_B10@1" * 0.00341802 + 149.0 - 273.15 (use your file's full layer name). Where is your study area hottest? Coolest?

Part E. Look at the quality band

  1. Add QA_PIXEL to the map. In Symbology, choose Paletted/Unique values and click Classify.
  2. Turn values on and off to see which ones match clouds, cloud shadows, water, and clear land in your natural-color composite. Take a screenshot.

What to submit

  • The full product name of your scene, with each part explained.
  • A figure with your three composites side by side, with two to three sentences on what each shows best.
  • A table of NIR reflectance for forest, water, and bare soil from Landsat, next to your Sentinel-2 values from Week 1. Explain any differences.
  • Your QA_PIXEL screenshot, with a note on how much of your study area is covered by cloud.

Check your understanding

1. What do you learn from the scene ID LC08_L2SP_016032_20230715_20230725_02_T1?

It is a Landsat 8 OLI/TIRS scene, processed to Level-2 surface reflectance and temperature, at WRS-2 path 16 and row 32. It was acquired on 15 July 2023, processed on 25 July 2023, belongs to Collection 2, and is Tier 1.

2. What is the difference between Level-1 and Level-2 products?

Level-1 products are calibrated and georeferenced but still include the effects of the atmosphere. Level-2 products are corrected for the atmosphere and give surface reflectance and surface temperature, which you can compare across dates.

3. A Level-2 NIR pixel has a stored value of 15,000. What is its surface reflectance?

15,000 × 0.0000275 − 0.2 = 0.4125 − 0.2 = 0.2125, or about 21% reflectance.

4. How often can you get a new Landsat image of the same place, using Landsat 8 and 9 together?

Every 8 days. Each satellite repeats every 16 days, and the two are offset by 8 days.

5. Why do Landsat 7 images after 2003 have stripes?

Landsat 7's Scan Line Corrector failed in 2003. Without it, the sensor leaves wedge-shaped gaps in every image.

6. Which composite would you use to quickly map healthy forest, and why?

Color infrared (bands 5, 4, 3). Healthy vegetation reflects strongly in the NIR, which this composite shows in red, so forests stand out in bright red.
